1. What is a Cluster Alpha?
On BRAIN, Alpha can be silently evaluated at two levels:
Stock level
— the usual view: a weight on each stock, and the Sharpe of the resulting PnL.
Cluster level
— BRAIN also aggregates your Alpha's exposures over
clusters
— a group of related instruments considered together, capturing broader common structure in the market such as related business activity, shared economic behavior, or similar market characteristics — and measures the performance of that aggregated book as a single
Cluster Sharpe
. If it clears the cutoff, the Alpha is tagged a
Cluster Alpha
.
The mental model:
collapse your Alpha to one net position per cluster
— the stocks in each group replaced by the group's aggregate exposure — and ask whether
that
cluster-level book is itself a good Alpha. If yes, you're a Cluster Alpha.

BRAIN's interpretation: Cluster Alphas "exhibit broader and more consistent patterns across related groups of instruments" — i.e. the badge rewards Alphas whose PnL has
real, consistent industry-level structure
, not single-name idiosyncrasy.
The design target of this tutorial: make the industry-level view of your Alpha a good Alpha.
Every idea below is judged by one question —
if I could only express positions in industries, would this make money?
2. What the test means for your research
You never "submit to" the Cluster Test.
It's computed automatically from your simulation. The only way to pass is for the industry-aggregated view of your Alpha to be genuinely strong.
It is orthogonal to the other submission tests.
Regular Sharpe, fitness, turnover, weight, sub-universe, and correlation tests all still apply — the badge relaxes nothing.
The bar depends on region.
KOR/JPS/TWN/HKG/IND/GBR/DEU need cluster-book Sharpe ≥ 1.0; elsewhere ≥ 1.58. Reference scale from research practice: converting a typical diversified equity book to the cluster level lands around
Sharpe 1.0–1.5
across major regions. So in 1.0-bar regions a strong group-tilted Alpha can qualify incidentally; in 1.58-bar regions you must have
deliberately built
a cluster-level technique.
3. BRAIN building blocks for industry research
3.1 The industry group field
The
industry
group field (in datasets such as
pv1
, findable in Data Explorer with the
type == 'group'
filter) assigns every stock its industry. It is the grouping this tutorial builds on throughout — industries are the natural, most economically meaningful cluster of "related business activity / shared economic behavior," which is exactly the structure the badge is designed to identify. (BRAIN also offers other groups — sector, subindustry, country — and lets you build custom ones with
bucket()
; they are legitimate tools, but industry is the workhorse for cluster research and the one used in every example below.)

3.4 The neutralization setting — the master knob
Industry / subindustry neutralization
subtracts the industry mean from your Alpha — it
deletes
industry-level structure by construction. An industry-neutral Alpha aggregates to nearly zero at the industry level. If you want a Cluster Alpha, this is the wrong setting.
Market (or no) neutralization
lets industry tilts survive into the aggregated book.
Decide first whether you are building an industry technique or a within-industry stock technique — they are close to opposite constructions.
4. The two core methodologies
4.1 Industry rotation — cross-sectional
Rank industries against each other and long the strong, short the weak. The signal is the
dispersion of industry strength
at each point in time.
# Industry momentum rotation: 6-month formation (the literature-standard window),
# cross-sectionally ranked
industry_mom = group_mean(ts_delta(close, 126), cap, industry);
rank(industry_mom)
Design rules (horizons from the literature — see §5.1):
Formation 6–12 months, holding 1–6 months
is where industry momentum is documented (Moskowitz & Grinblatt 1999); the effect is robust to skipping a month between formation and holding.
Aggregate with a sensible weight (
cap
for size-aware industries; equal for pure breadth — equal-weighting roughly doubles the documented effect, see §5.1).
Guard thin industries: multiply or filter by
group_count(x, industry) > 5
— a 3-stock "industry" is noise, not structure.
The mechanism inside
group_mean
can be anything with industry-level meaning — price momentum, diffusion signals, fundamental aggregates, analyst revisions (see §5).
4.2 Industry timing — time-series
Instead of ranking industries against each other, decide
when
each industry (or the industry book as a whole) deserves a long, short, or flat position. The signal is the
time-series state
of each industry.
# Industry timing: hold an industry only while its own aggregate trend is positive
industry_ret = group_mean(returns, cap, industry);
signal = ts_mean(industry_ret, 20);
rank(signal)                        # cross-industry scoring of trend strength
Time-series ingredients that fit naturally on industry aggregates:
Trend / regime
:
ts_mean(industry_ret, d)
or
ts_delta
of the industry aggregate — positive-trend industries long, negative-trend short. The documented version is time-series momentum:
12-month lookback, sign of the trend, inverse-volatility position scaling
(Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen 2012 — the diversified trend book runs at Sharpe > 1, see §5.3).
Abnormality
:
ts_zscore(industry_ret, d)
— industries in an abnormal state.
Risk-off filters
: gate exposure with
trade_when
— e.g. only express the rotation outside "panic states" (bear market × high volatility), which is where momentum books crash (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016 — state-conditioning roughly doubles momentum's Sharpe, see §5.3).
Rotation × timing combination
(usually the strongest): rotate across industries, but let the time-series state of each industry scale or gate its position —
rank(group_mean(ts_delta(close, 126), cap, industry)) * (ts_mean(industry_ret, 20) > 0)
.
Note the difference in what survives aggregation: pure rotation is dollar-neutral across industries at all times; timing lets the industry book take net direction per industry over time. Both are industry books — timing usually needs the extra care on turnover.
5. Idea library — from the research literature
Every idea here answers "would this make money if I could only express positions in industries?" first, and is anchored in a citable result: mechanism → BRAIN construction → what the paper found.
5.1 Industry momentum — the canonical cluster effect
Moskowitz & Grinblatt (1999), "Do Industries Explain Momentum?",
Journal of Finance
.
Industries that outperformed over the past 6–12 months continue to outperform over the next 1–6 months, and this industry effect accounts for much of individual-stock momentum.
# Industry momentum: 6-month formation (126 market days), cross-industry rank
rank(group_mean(ts_delta(close, 126), cap, industry))
What the paper found, and what it means for construction:
0.43%/month
value-weighted;
0.81%/month
with
equal-weighted
industries — on BRAIN this is the
group_normalize
corollary from §3.3: equal-weighting industries is not cosmetic, it nearly doubles the effect.
Long-side driven
(winners-minus-middle 0.36%/mo vs middle-minus-losers 0.07%/mo) — the effect survives without aggressive shorts.
Robust to
skipping a month
between formation and holding.
Two counterpoints to know:
Grundy & Martin (2001)
find momentum built on
industry-adjusted
stock returns performs better than total-return momentum (stock-specific and industry effects are different bets — pick yours deliberately);
Arnott, Kalesnik & Linnainmaa (2023, "Factor Momentum", RFS)
show factor momentum
subsumes
industry momentum — know that an industry momentum book loads on broader cross-sectional momentum.
5.2 What NOT to build: short-term industry reversal
Da, Liu & Schaumburg (2014), "A Closer Look at the Short-Term Return Reversal",
Management Science
.
The 1-month reversal decomposes into an
across-industry component that is negative on average
(because industries exhibit momentum) and a
within-industry
component that carries all the returns.
Do not
fade industries at short horizons (
rank(-group_mean(returns, cap, industry))
at monthly scale has negative expectancy per this paper — short-term, industries
continue
, they don't snap back).
The reversal effect that pays is
within-industry
— which, by §3.3, is a different concept from a Cluster Alpha (it neutralizes the very dimension you'd be aggregating).
Their instrument is worth stealing: they condition on
analyst forecast revisions
to separate fundamental from non-fundamental price moves (see §5.6).
5.3 Industry timing — trend, vol scaling, crash states
Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012), "Time Series Momentum",
JFE
.
An asset's own past 12-month excess return sign tends to predict its next-month return — directly implementable as industry timing:
# TSMOM at industry level: long industries above their own 12m trend, short below,
# scaled inversely to each industry's ex-ante volatility
industry_ret = group_mean(returns, cap, industry);
trend = ts_mean(industry_ret, 250);
vol   = ts_std_dev(industry_ret, 60);
rank(trend / vol)
The diversified time-series-momentum book runs at
Sharpe > 1
with vol scaling — the scaling is part of the result, not an afterthought.
Daniel & Moskowitz (2016), "Momentum Crashes",
JFE
.
Momentum books (industry rotation included) crash in "panic states" — after market declines, when volatility is high, contemporaneous with rebounds. Conditioning exposure on the state (market's prior 2-year return × current vol) roughly
doubles the technique's Sharpe
. On BRAIN this is the
trade_when
regime gate from §4.2, now with a citation for why it works:
# Panic-state gate: express the rotation only outside bear-market × high-vol states
rotation = rank(group_mean(ts_delta(close, 126), cap, industry));
panic = (ts_mean(returns, 500) < 0) && (ts_std_dev(returns, 60) > ts_mean(ts_std_dev(returns, 60), 250));
trade_when(!panic, rotation, -1)
5.4 Diffusion-based rotation — information travels between industries
The most distinctive family: instead of an industry's
own
past return, use the returns of
related
industries.
Menzly & Ozbas (2010), "Market Segmentation and Cross-predictability of Returns",
JF
.
Information diffuses gradually along the supply chain: returns of supplier industries tend to predict returns of their customer industries (and vice versa), at momentum horizons, distinct from own- industry momentum. Construction: rank industries on the past 6-month return of their economically-linked counterparties, not their own.
Cohen & Frazzini (2008), "Economic Links and Predictable Returns",
JF
.
The firm-level version: customer returns tend to predict supplier returns at 1–2 month horizons (>150 bps/month in long/short form) because attention-constrained investors underreact to news about related firms. At industry scale, the same link matrix is a rotation input.
Hong, Torous & Valkanov (2007), "Do Industries Lead Stock Markets?",
JFE
.
Some industries (retail, services, commercial real estate, metals, petroleum) lead the aggregate market by up to
two months
— an early-rotation/timing signal for the whole industry book.
Hou (2007), "Industry Information Diffusion and the Lead-Lag Effect",
RFS
.
Large-firm (industry-leader) returns tend to predict small-firm (follower) returns
within
the industry, and this intra-industry lead-lag is what drives industry momentum — an industry aggregate built from leader performance leads the full-industry aggregate.
5.5 Macro-driven rotation (classical form)
Conover, Jensen, Johnson & Mercer (2008), "Sector Rotation and Monetary Conditions",
Journal of Investing
.
Monetary-policy direction tends to predict the cyclical/defensive spread: overweight cyclicals during easing, defensives during tightening. Infrequent (annual-ish) rebalancing; the benefit concentrates in poor equity markets.
Polk, Haghbin & de Longis (2020), "Time-Series Variation in Factor Premia", SSRN 3377677.
Business-cycle regimes from leading indicators + risk appetite tend to predict which styles/sectors outperform next; dynamic rotation delivers information ratios
~2× static
allocation.
On BRAIN these become slow overlay variables: a regime dummy (or a continuous tilt) multiplying the industry book, with cyclicals/defensives classified once via their data field profiles.
Recent practitioner work pushes the same direction with ML:
arXiv 2304.09947 (2023)
builds online-ensemble sector rotation on the documented premise that
sector returns tend to be more predictable and more stable than individual asset returns
— independent, literature-level support for cluster research itself.
5.6 Industry-aggregated information (the data side)
Don't aggregate only prices — aggregate an
informational dataset
to the industry and express an industry position with it:
Analyst revision breadth
: fraction of upward revisions per industry — industries whose analysts collectively upgrade tend to keep outperforming; revisions are also the instrument Da-Liu-Schaumburg use to separate fundamental from non-fundamental moves.
Fundamental aggregates
: industry-level earnings growth, margin change —
rank(group_mean(fundamental_growth_field, cap, industry))
.
Sentiment — an honest null
:
Molchanov & Stangl (2018), "Investor Sentiment and Industry Returns"
find sentiment affects industries
market-wide
but differentiates them poorly cross-sectionally — a sentiment-based industry
timing
technique has little merit. Don't expect industry-level sentiment dispersion to be a rotation signal.
The averaging inside
group_mean
does real work here: it strips single-stock noise out of a noisy dataset and leaves the industry-level information — a favorable balance for Cluster Sharpe.
5.7 Clusterize an existing Alpha at industry level
Have a stock-level Alpha that works? Re-express it as an industry book:
clusterized = group_mean(your_signal, cap, industry);
rank(clusterized)
Every stock carries its industry's average signal — the book collapses to one position per industry. Expect
lower
Sharpe than the original (fewer effective bets — tens of industries vs thousands of stocks) but
lower correlation too
: research practice puts the correlation of an industry-clusterized book to its original around
~0.5
— the healthy zone (real industry structure, not a clone). Refinements: filter industries whose members mostly disagree (low signal-value conviction), and equal-weight industries with
group_normalize
when member counts are lopsided.
5.8 Cluster-aware stock Alpha (badge as a byproduct — for 1.0-bar regions)
Keep a stock-selection Alpha, but construct it so its PnL aggregates well: use mechanisms whose economics operate at industry level, do NOT industry-neutralize (§3.4), avoid idiosyncrasy (special situations, name events — they wash out under industry aggregation). Quick self-diagnostic:
group_mean(signal, cap, industry)
— if the industry-aggregated signal has cross-sectional spread and economic meaning, the Alpha is cluster-compatible. In KOR/JPS/TWN/HKG/IND/GBR/DEU (bar 1.0) this path often clears the badge without any cluster-specific engineering.
6. Evaluation loop and pitfalls
Loop:
build → check regular submission stats (nothing is relaxed) →
self-check the industry level
: aggregate your Alpha to industries, compute the industry book's Sharpe yourself (or proxy it: the Sharpe of
rank(group_mean(your_signal, cap, industry))
as a standalone Alpha), compare to 1.0/1.58 → check correlation of the industry book vs your original and vs your other submissions (self-corr < 0.7, or 10% Sharpe improvement, still applies).
Pitfalls:
Overfitting is the primary risk.
An industry book has tens of effective bets (≈ the number of industries), not thousands. Prefer industry-level economic mechanisms over tuned lookbacks. In the internal program's history, cluster books degraded from in-sample ~1.3 to out-sample ~0.7 on average — build margin above the bar.
Thin industries are noise.
Always guard with
group_count
.
Neutralization mismatch.
Industry-neutralizing a rotation/timing idea is self-defeating; check §3.4 before simulating.
Timing turnover.
Time-series gates (
trade_when
, trend conditions) can spike turnover — smooth the industry aggregates (
ts_decay_linear
) before gating.
Don't chase the badge at the cost of the real tests.
Only build industry structure when it is also the economic idea.
Region matters twice
: the bar (1.0 vs 1.58) and the industry field's granularity per region (check Data Explorer; ~70 industries typical).
